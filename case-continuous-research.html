CONTINUOUS USER RESEARCH

The research loop that turned a technical migration into a workflow readiness question.

ROLE
Senior product designer, financial workflows lead
TEAM
Co-owned with Sandra, PM support from Ninon and Christian
CADENCE
A two week cycle, still running
CONTEXT
Unified Platform migration

Nordhealth was migrating therapy customers from legacy Desktop products to a newer web platform. The migration dashboard could tell us whether records moved. It could not tell us whether a practitioner could still run a full clinical day without losing speed, confidence, or context. So we built a standing research loop with two practitioners to surface workflow risk before it became a rollout blocker.

The old question Did the data migrate?

The question we shipped on Can practitioners still do their work?

WHAT A DASHBOARD CANNOT SHOW

A migration looks binary from the inside. Accounts and records either moved or they did not. From a treatment room it feels nothing like that. Appointments, documentation, patient context, invoicing, and follow-up all still have to happen between patients, at the same speed as yesterday.

That is where the risk hides. A practitioner can accept the idea of a new platform and still lose confidence the moment a familiar task takes longer, sits somewhere new, or uses a word they do not recognise. Small gaps compound into frustration, support tickets, and stalled adoption. Every one of those is a migration that slows down, or a customer who starts looking elsewhere.

TWO PRACTITIONERS, ON PURPOSE

We set up recurring sessions with two practitioner profiles who represent very different clinical days: Andre, a physiotherapist, and Eyvind, a psychologist. Each cycle took the current product questions to them: what was about to change, what had just shipped, and where we suspected a gap.

The goal was never statistical representation. It was continuity. Returning to the same two people every cycle let us watch confidence move: whether a workflow that confused them in March had settled by May, and whether each new wave of migrated work made their day better or worse.

Migration risk lives in the details, not the decision.

Neither practitioner objected to moving platforms in principle. Confidence dropped around specifics: a shortcut that no longer existed, a field that had moved, a task that now took four clicks instead of one. Those are invisible in migration metrics and obvious within thirty seconds of watching someone work.

A RHYTHM, NOT A STUDY

A one-off validation study gives you a snapshot, and migration is not a snapshot. Workflows land in the new platform in waves, so a session in March cannot tell you about the risk arriving in May. We designed an operating rhythm instead, and protected half of it for thinking.

In week A the team reviews the topic backlog and picks the most urgent questions. Sandra and I prepare lightweight material and share it with the practitioners in advance, then run the sessions later that week, each of us leading the part closest to our domain. Week B is synthesis in Dovetail, a short summary to product and design, async follow-ups in Slack, and new questions going back into the backlog.

HOW THE LOOP WORKED

  1. 01 CAPTURE UNCERTAINTY

    Topics arrive from support tickets, onboarding surveys, PostHog, previous sessions, roadmap gaps in Linear, and CS or sales conversations.

  2. 02 PRIORITISE THE BACKLOG

    Every other Monday the team picks one to three topics by urgency and migration risk. Anyone can add to the list.

  3. 03 PREPARE LIGHTLY

    Focused prompts, a prototype, or a staging task, depending on what the question needs. Never a script.

  4. 04 SIT WITH PRACTITIONERS

    Andre and Eyvind walk their real workflows, react to designs, test builds, and say what has annoyed them since last time.

  5. 05 SYNTHESISE AND SHARE

    Analysis in Dovetail, then a short summary to product and design. Decision support, not research theatre.

  6. 06 FEED THE NEXT CYCLE

    Clarifications continue in Slack. New questions become backlog topics, and the loop turns again.

  7. BACK TO 01, EVERY TWO WEEKS

FOUR RISKS, SIX WAYS TO ASK

Every session pointed at one of four risks, so findings could be compared across cycles rather than collected as anecdotes.

  • MIGRATION GAPS What is missing, broken, or harder to complete than it was in Physica or Psykbase?
  • WORKFLOW FIT Can practitioners still finish their core work at clinical speed?
  • FEATURE READINESS Does upcoming work match real needs before we spend engineering on it?
  • ADOPTION FRICTION Where do people get stuck, confused, or pushed into extra effort?

The backlog let us move between strategic and tactical questions. One cycle asked whether the new sidebar supported daily navigation. Another tested group appointment scoping, booking statuses, invoice creation, or the split between clinical and accounting reports before those decisions hardened.

The session format followed the question rather than a template: workflow walkthroughs to see real work, discovery conversations to surface unknowns, design reviews before engineering investment, feature testing in staging or behind flags, general feedback to track sentiment, and async video when something was easier to record than to describe.

DECISIONS BEHIND THE PROCESS

A standing rhythm, not ad hoc research
Migration risk arrives in instalments as more workflows move. A fixed slot means the team can raise a question on Monday and have practitioner evidence the same week.
A backlog, not a script
Anyone can add a topic the moment uncertainty appears. Choosing one to three per cycle keeps sessions aimed at what is live, instead of a plan written weeks ago.
Domain ownership, shared view
Sandra led clinical workflows, I led financial ones, each of us owning prep, facilitation, and synthesis in our area. Splitting the depth kept the sessions sharp while the whole team still saw one user journey.
Processing time is part of the system
Week B exists so synthesis is not something that happens if there is time. It is why findings land while product and migration decisions are still open.
Lightweight outputs, fast
Dovetail notes, a short summary, a Slack thread. A polished report that arrives after the call has been made is worth nothing.

WHAT CHANGED

The loop moved migration readiness from a technical question to a workflow question. Instead of asking only whether data had moved, the team could ask whether a practitioner could still complete the work that matters: appointments, documentation, patient context, invoicing, follow-up, and daily navigation.

  • Workflow gaps surfaced weeks before rollout instead of arriving as support tickets.
  • Roadmap work was validated against real practitioner workflows before engineering spend.
  • Features were tested in staging or behind flags while changes were still cheap.
  • Product decisions arrived with practitioner evidence attached instead of internal opinion.
  • Design, product, CS, and migration teams built one shared picture of the migrated user.

FROM FINDING TO DECISION

Invoice status visibility came into the backlog from the financial side as a low priority question: could practitioners see enough about where an insurance invoice stood? In isolation it reads like a small usability gap. In context it decides whether someone trusts that they have been paid, and whether they go hunting through another system to check.

WHAT I WOULD IMPROVE

Two practitioners is a thin sample, and I would not defend it as representative. It was a deliberate trade: depth and continuity with people who trusted us enough to be blunt, over a broader study that would have arrived once and too late. The next version widens the loop to more practitioner types and tracks each insight from discovery through to resolution, so the process can show its own hit rate.

The wider lesson: in a platform migration, design is not only redesigning screens. It is designing the feedback system that tells the organisation whether the change is actually safe to ship.
