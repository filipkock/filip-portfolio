P-01 · CASE STUDY

AI JOURNALING ASSISTANT

Less time writing notes. More time with patients.

ROLE
Lead product designer + front-end dev
TEAM
Me + one full-stack developer
TIMELINE
6 weeks, 2025
PLATFORM
Web app

Therapists told us documentation was the one thing standing between them and better patient care: every session ended in unpaid minutes of typing. I designed and helped build an AI assistant that listens to the session and drafts the clinical note, inside the journal they already use.

TL;DR

Clinical documentation was the top time sink for therapists on our platform, and AI-first startups were circling the workflow. In six weeks we shipped an MVP that records a session, transcribes it, and drafts a structured SOAP note the therapist reviews before saving. Usage tripled to 1,500 generated notes a day, 500+ customers across Norway, Finland, and Denmark now pay for it, and a Norway pilot showed 92.9% positive sentiment toward AI in journaling.

THE PROBLEM

A psychologist finishes a heavy 50-minute session. Before the next patient arrives, they reconstruct it from memory into a compliant journal entry, then maybe an epicrisis and a sick note from the same material. Customers named this their number one time sink, and worse than the time itself: it pulled attention away from the patient in the room.

For the business the stakes were commercial. Documentation drag limited clinic capacity, and standalone AI scribes threatened to own this high-value workflow first, then expand into ours. An embedded assistant could make the platform more compelling, support migrations, reduce churn, and open a new paid add-on. Winning meant real daily usage, not a demo feature.

DISCOVERY & INSIGHT

I interviewed therapists, mapped their documentation workflows end to end, mined existing customer feedback, and audited the AI-first competitors. The pattern was clear: those tools produced decent text but lived outside the therapist's world, so every note meant switching apps, copying, and reformatting.

We didn't need a better transcription tool. We needed no tool at all.

We already ran the calendar, the journal, and the billing. So the assistant had to feel embedded, fast, editable, and unmistakably under the therapist's control - and the transcript should be a reusable asset, not a one-shot output.

THE APPROACH

We had a hard MVP deadline, legacy journal workflows we couldn't break, GDPR and consent requirements, and real limits around audio capture, transcription quality, latency, and browser support. So I cut ruthlessly: embedded note generation with editable SOAP summaries first. Patient-history context, confidence highlighting, speaker diarization, and multi-document workflows were designed as directions, not deliverables.

I also designed a standalone version of the same engine, so the assistant could be sold on its own and grow into later features without rework.

DESIGN DECISIONS

Live inside the journal
Therapists should never leave their primary workflow mid-session. Embedding the assistant where notes already live removed the switching cost that made competitors feel like homework.
Spend clicks like money
The product's entire value was time. Every step from calendar to saved note was audited; anything that didn't build trust or control was cut.
Editable by default
Clinical accuracy is the therapist's legal responsibility. Output always lands as a draft in an open editor, which turned skeptics into users: the AI proposes, the clinician disposes.
SOAP first
One familiar, structured format gave the MVP a shape every therapist recognized, kept scope shippable in six weeks, and left custom templates as a natural next step.

IMPACT

  • ~200 sign-ups from the Oslo launch event: 40 on day one of the conference, 80 more from the pre-announcement email.
  • Usage tripled from 550 to 1,500 AI-generated notes per day.
  • 500+ paying customers across Norway, Finland, and Denmark, validating the paid add-on model.
  • 92.9% positive sentiment toward AI in journaling in the Norway pilot survey.

REFLECTION

The need was real, and shipping fast proved it. But the first version was rushed, and it showed: we launched before proper validation, and early feedback called out weak AI output and too many clicks to start a session - exactly what deeper research would have caught. The lesson I carry forward is that an MVP still has to be genuinely viable, and shipping is the start of the research, not the end of it.

Since then the assistant has outgrown its MVP: a standalone experience, dictation, an AI co-pilot for journaling, broader templates, handwritten note digitization, and more markets. Custom templates and deeper contextual assistance are next, currently paused for other priorities but expected to resume.
